### 1. A clear night, for once. 

It is a clear night at the beach. Cloudless nights are not common on the coast in the summer. The city lights have not completely washed out the Milky Way. The moon is below the horizon so the stars are clear. I read an article once about astronomy (or did I see on the tube?) about the Aborigines in Australia. They view the night sky in a totally different way than I do. When I look at the sky, I see all the constellations, groups of stars that were so important to the ancient peoples of our hemisphere. The Aborigines do the opposite: they regard the dark shapes of the gas and dust in the galactic plane as more interesting[^1]. As I look up, pareidolia helps me make out some familiar shapes, like clouds on a bright day. The shapes seem to be drifting in a river of stars. The sound of the waves crashing on the sand adds more depth to the view. 

I can only imagine what it was like to see the gas, dust, and stars, and not really know truly what they are. I have the benefit of thousands of years of study by many different cultures. I can go to the library and check out a book that explains exactly everything we know up to this point about the universe around us. Leaving out certain religions with with certain books, human history shows that not everything, even here on our planet, has an explanation. We do know quite a lot about our physical environment. We know what we are to some degree, we just don't know why. It turns out, asking questions is way more interesting than getting answers. 

### 2. Curiosity and walking sacks of meat (now, with less hair!). 

First there was fire, then the wheel. Layered clothing? Yes, please. It gets chilly in the northern climes and we don’t seem to have as much hair as we used to. Better start inventing weapons and killing more animals for their skins and food. In between all the hunting, gathering and killing, we moved giant stones, drew lines in the desert and began to track the stars. We hopped on that agriculture train and took off. Today, we are inventing machines and giant telescopes that can see farther into space and time than our eyes can.

Our heads are so big now. In the 1970s, astronomers noticed that galaxies were rotating differently than expected. There must be something, they thought, that was causing the galaxies to act more massive than they were. A placeholder with specific properties had to be thought of in order to make the math work. The concept of [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter) has been hypothesized for many decades, and the more of the universe we can see, the more we can confirm its existence. When we finally detect and measure it, we will have even more questions. Well then which is more important, the searching or the answers? The universe is a bounty of questions.

Who in the hell do we think we are, anyway? How can a bunch of hairy sacks of meat living on an insignificant rock comprehend the true nature of the universe? We can think big and create wild abstract notions, but can our tiny brains really handle the true answers? I posit that the universe is so vast, we will never find what we are truly looking for. Maybe we will one day build a machine that can find the answers. Humans have been known to spend ridiculous amounts of money and plunder many resources to achieve a goal. Telescopes, space centers, satellites. We are creating computers that can solve problems millions of times faster than our brains are able to. 

In one of my favorite analogies of human behavior, [_The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phrases_from_The_Hitchhiker%27s_Guide_to_the_Galaxy), Author Douglas Adams writes about a massive computer that was built to answer the ultimate question about life, the universe, and everything. After millions of years of computation, it spits out the answer: 

>"All right," said the computer, and settled into silence again. The two men fidgeted. The tension was unbearable.  
>"You're really not going to like it," observed Deep Thought.  
>"Tell us!"   
>"All right," said Deep Thought. "The Answer to the Great Question..."  
>"Yes..!"  
>"Of Life, the Universe and Everything..." said Deep Thought.  
>"Yes...!"  
>"Is..." said Deep Thought, and paused.  
>"Yes...!"  
>"Is..."  
>"Yes...!!!...?"  
>"Forty-two," said Deep Thought, with infinite majesty and calm.  
>‑ Douglas Adams, _The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy_. 

The answer is incomprehensible because the question is misunderstood.[^2]. Even though the characters in the story are not necessarily human, the analogy rings true: humans ask questions that are too complicated to understand. 

### 3. Beyond the ether toward the infinite. 

What exists outside our atmosphere has had many definitions over the short time humans have thought about it. There is so much mystery out there. Indeed, before we were sure the planet was round, there were descriptions and stories from every culture. Firmaments and floods, gods and goddesses, birds poking holes in the night sky with their beaks. We filled traditions and myths with abstract explanations using things that were familiar to us. Long after humans recorded the planets, they had the sun at the center of the solar system. Then it moved into orbit around the Earth. Then it moved back. The Earth switched from flat to round many times. At first, the planets were lonely wanderers[^3], but then their moons were discovered. The ether, it seemed was still out there until the early twentieth century. Time and matter was relative. The universe began to look less like a clockworks and more like an endless chaos where light doesn't experience time, yet we can see billions of years into the past by looking up on a clear night.
 
The shapes the Aborigines see and the furthest galaxies have one thing in common: they pose more questions than they answer. When astronomers and physicists answer one question, they can press on, get deeper into the details. There is a luminous radio source at the center of the Milky Way, what is it? Is it a [black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagittarius_A) or something even weirder? Now what? The probability of us going to see any of this in person is very remote. But we can try to make better telescopes, use more advanced computers to render simulations, and maybe we can someday get a good picture of it, whatever the hell it is. No matter how hard we look, no matter what part of the electromagnetic spectrum we use, the observable universe is all we get to see. The universe will hold mysteries that will outlast us long after we become stardust. 

### 4. The beautiful asymptote of human knowledge. 

Naturally, our species tends to take all these smaller questions and lump them into bigger questions. The bigger questions require bigger answers. Since the turn of the twentieth century, we also discovered that we have limits to what we can do and where we can go. I'm not sure if anyone is bothering to ask if going somewhere far away will result in an understandable answer to our big questions. Seeing a quasar in the Andromeda galaxy in person probably won't tell us why we exist. But imagine the journey to that quasar in Andromeda. I think that would be way more meaningful than the quasar itself. That is, unless we get there and it turns out to be a psychedelic freak show.

Let's imagine for a moment that we use a wormhole or other method to explore strange new worlds. We’re be-boppin' and scattin' all over the galaxy, measuring gases, finding habitable planets, and getting all kinds of questions answered. The shear size of the universe is so ridiculously large, we cannot possibly cope with the scale, even in the abstract. We will merely continue along, answering small questions, getting ever closer to the truth. The path to the truth is asymptotic: we will approach but never fully reach _total_ understanding. If this sounds like a giant bummer, I disagree. Just think about the fun we will have doing all that exploring. Who cares about the asymptote when there is so much fun to be had on the ride? 

### 5. Just give it up, already. 

Is nihilism our fate? Many find it doubtful that we will ever travel faster than light. Even if we someday manage it, how can we tell the folks back on Earth what we found? According to general relativity, the people we know now will be millennia gone by the time we get the center of the Milky Way. Andromeda? Forget it. We may be stuck here. Stuck within the [Oort Cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oort_cloud), traveling years to mine a few asteroids or comets. Maybe we will find signs of alien life in one of our solar system's cosmic interlopers (crap, _more questions!_). 

To us, the Earth seems smaller these days. We can talk to anyone we want, instantly. We can be anywhere on the globe within the span of a day. But let’s not get too far ahead of ourselves. Our [Pale Blue Dot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pale_Blue_Dot) is bigger and holds more mysteries than we think it does. Much of the world's oceans have not been mapped or explored. The problems of climate change, war, and poverty demand solutions. And what, really, is going on _inside_ our planet?

Besides the fate of our race probably not surviving the destruction of our own planet, I can imagine the terrible fates that will befoul our meat-sack species. The first is realizing and accepting that we will never find the answers. The second is actually finding some of the answers and ceasing to search and explore. Giving up, it seems, is not a good idea. Let's press on, shall we? 

### 6. Now we are just making stuff up.

Why not just make up the answers and pretend we know it all? Evolution blessed us with intelligence. So it is ironic that we used our brainpower (not very much of it, in my opinion) to conjure up religion. Unfortunately for many of the thirty billion people that came before us, apathy proved too difficult to overcome. Some humans find it a bother to ask questions. They prefer to gather in clumps and _decide_ that all questions have already been answered. These answers usually come in the form of [holy books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible). When faced with difficult questions, they do not have to spend anymore energy than a flick of the finger to find an answer written by some weirdo long ago. There you have it: no further effort is required.

When I mentioned books full of answers, is that not a little hypocritical? Don't the books and papers written by scientists innumerable say the same thing? Well, scientific exploration has one thing religion does not: _parity_. For every scientific paper there is another that refutes, modifies, or adds to it. There is also the review and replication by the scientific community to accept or reject ideas. No such parity exists in the holy texts (at least in my particular upbringing). Can you imagine a bunch of scientists trying to replicate the loaves and the fishes? No, it's totally made up. Curing [leprosy](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/leprosy) on the other hand, we know is now possible, just not through any divine miracle.

The oft-quoted [Albert Einstein](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Albert_Einstein) sometimes used religious pretext to describe his own experiences with the universe. For this, people thought that he was religious and was himself confused. He also answered a lot of letters:

>It was, of course, a lie what you read about my religious convictions, a lie which is being systematically repeated. I do not believe in a personal God and I have never denied this but have expressed it clearly. If something is in me which can be called religious then it is the unbounded admiration for the structure of the world so far as our science can reveal it.  
>‑ Albert Einstein. Letter to an atheist (24 March 1954), p. 43.

If that was at all vague, here's another:

>I have never imputed to Nature a purpose or a goal, or anything that could be understood as anthropomorphic. What I see in Nature is a magnificent structure that we can comprehend only very imperfectly, and that must fill a thinking person with a feeling of "humility." This is a genuinely religious feeling that has nothing to do with mysticism.  
>‑ Albert Einstein. Draft of a German reply to a letter sent to him in 1954 or 1955, p. 39.

This feeling of humility is a perfect substitute for a feeling of awe or wonder. Whether the unadventurous believe in an intelligent creator of the universe matters not. The universe is _all_ there is. By that I mean there is so much there, that even a figment of creative imagination from billions of human minds cannot fully comprehend the enormity of it all. There will always be more to learn. Divine books have an [ending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Revelation). The universe does not.

### 7. Approaching a conclusion. 

Tumbling through space and time. Cruising from planet to planet. Mining asteroids and catching comets. Our solar system is our home. Despite this, we shall keep sending probes into the void. Maybe a space-faring civilization will come and see us sometime. Maybe they will have some answers for us. Until then, we shall continue making things up as we go, as we do. As my father always said: curiosity only kills cats.

And after millennia drift by, past the heat death of our universe, we will still probably never be satisfied. Maybe we will survive past the destruction of Earth. Maybe we will explore space and transcend time and the universe itself. After all is explored and there is nothing left, we will always have ourselves. To wit:  

>The lights were soft, the moment was right. Then she leaned over and whispered in his ear, 'Do you love me?' Thoughts raced through his mind. Did she really want him? What had he done to deserve this bounty? Does God exist? Who invented liquid soap and why?  
>‑ The Sure Thing (1985)

If we decided to give up, decided to stop moving forward, we would probably die.
