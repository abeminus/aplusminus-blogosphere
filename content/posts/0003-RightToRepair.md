###  A story of two skill sets and hard-earned money.

My father was an electronics engineer who loved music. He loved playing it, recording it, and collecting it. The house I grew up in had shelves packed with vinyl records, cassette tapes, and reel-to-reel tapes. His car served as a transport vehicle for boxes of 8-tracks. The confluence of his career and his passion made sense to me only later in my life. He could fix anything electronic: rotary phones, tube amplifiers, tape heads, turntable motors, anything. 

He was able to modify and repair his beloved audio equipment because he had the skills. More importantly, he paid good money for it. That equipment was his. Thanks Section 1201 of the [Digital Millennium Copyright Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Millennium_Copyright_Act), companies are protecting their equipment as if it were their property even after you purchased it.

Most people don’t realize this, but there was a time when many electronics manufacturers made their devices easy to repair if they broke. In many cases, they _expected_ their customers to open up the machines themselves, or take them to a repair shop. I can recall several pieces of my dad’s things with _circuit diagrams on the inside of their covers._

### The olden days.

Consumers are finding that their ability to modify or repair many of the products they have purchased is no longer legal. They are fighting back. It is called the [Right-to-Repair Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_to_repair), and it’s a shame that we have to have a name for it. After thousands of years of exchanging money for something, we have reached a point where greed has redefined the concept of ownership.

In 2007, a man named [George Hotz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Hotz) removed the SIM lock on an iPhone in order to use any phone service for the device. When Apple released the first iPhone, there was only one choice for a carrier, AT&T. Mr. Hotz then continued his exploration into modifying devices, eventually releasing software and instructions on the internet. Apple was not a fan of this activity.

However, Mr. Hotz was not releasing Apple’s trade secret to the world through this action. He was giving tools to people so they, too, could modify the devices that they _paid_ for. Though at the time, Apple did nothing about this.

Mr. Hotz eventually faced legal action for his curiosity. In 2010, Sony filed suit against him for modifying and advertising sever key components for jailbreaking the PlayStation 3. Sony went so far as to try and hunt down those who read his posts and sue them as well. Talk about overreach.

But Sony had nothing to fear from this. Yes, they have shareholders and news about hacking of their flagship console would affect their bottom line, but only a little. Still it would be enough to make investors worry. It also would damage the company’s ability to keep their consumers in their ecosystem. I still wonder, how many people would actually take the time to try and install Linux on a PS3? Enough to make a dent in sales? Not at all. I’m sure even Sony knows that most of their customers just want to play games. 

### Welcome to the suck.

But it still leaves a bad taste in your mouth, doesn’t it? Sony is the same company that [_removed_ previously purchased content](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=krXH8jXefqE) because they no longer had a license to distribute it. Sony is just one example. Amazon has done similar thing by restricting the downloads of your _purchased_ Kindle books, and Netflix has also pulled the rug out from many of its customers by suddenly restricting password sharing (they used to encourage it).

Where does that leave the consumers? It leaves them in a place where they have little choice but to find a better experience. It’s not easy. All the competition is treating everyone the same way. Nintendo and Xbox? Same. Hulu and Disney? Same. The only other ebook retailer that is worth anything right now is Rakuten, and they haven’t fallen in line with Amazon, yet. And don’t even get me started on Spotify and Apple Music. 

I guess it’s piracy, right. Right now, many people are giving the content providers of the world a huge middle finger. Sailing the high seas for the right to own their material, play it where they want on as many screens or devices as they wish. There will be a tipping point where companies will either a) give in and be a lot nicer to their customers, or b) start building even larger ecosystems, fencing everybody in, and suing their customers on a mass scale for terrible acts like wanting to keep an ebook after the license expires. God forbid.

### Examples from the past.

This isn’t a new phenomenon. The fight over digital content really started to gain mainstream attention with [Napster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Napster). Once people had a CD burner in their computer, they could get the songs out of their libraries and onto the internet for sharing. Soon, many people were straight up stealing music to such a degree, that it actually affected the bottom line of the music industry. The most famous music industry retaliations against Napster was a suit brought against them by Metallica. _Metallica_ for chrissakes! 

Going back even further, I can recall when the music industry was freaking out over blank cassette tapes. They were worried that if people could record music at home, and make their own music mixes, the whole industry would collapse. The thing is, to get a clean recording, one had to buy the album first. Radio stations always cut into the beginnings and endings of songs to discourage recording. But if I _paid_ for the album, shouldn”t I hae had the right to make a copy? And where would people like me be without the ability to make a mixtape for a girl I had a crush on?  

### But what about the companies!

I can _feel_ some of you rolling your eyes and saying “but the companies have a right to dictate what their consumers do with their product. If you don’t like it, don’t buy it!” I guess one could argue that, to a certain degree, companies need to protect their intellectual property. They certainly have the right to try and prevent a consumer from revealing their little secrets to other companies. Right? However, copying and modifying is not the same as corporate espionage. Besides, by the time a consumer uncovers a secret, most companies will have already been in on it.

### Conclusion.

It’s hard to imagine, back in the 1970s, telling someone that they were not allowed to modify their stereo. It would be absurd. hether a company produces entertainment, hardware, or software, they need consumers to pay for it. It only makes sense that after they part with their hard-earned money, they own the item they bought, right? Selling a product to a consumer for a profit is, after all, the whole point of capitalism. But I guess the base tenet of the capitalist ideal is not enough any more. They are trying to get every single cent out of us at any cost. On purpose.
