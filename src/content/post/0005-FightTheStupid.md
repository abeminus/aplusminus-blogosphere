### Introduction

The world is full of evil. The world is also full of idiots, jerks, assholes, fools, ignoramuses, and as we will see, people who are just plain stupid. Most of us will, upon learning the exploits of others, be quick to judge. It is in our nature to resist those things that we perceive as a danger to our survival. At the time I am writing this, it has never been easier to take a side. Thanks to the democratization of information, our lovely global village is now thriving on engagement and conflict. So what happened to our capacity for understanding? We are humans. We are better than this. We have the ability to understand and think.

Understanding is an important function of critical thinking, even if you disagree with something. To be fair, it takes time and energy to do this kind of work with your brain. Most people choose not to, deciding instead to disagree, take sides, or hate. Evil and stupidity have given many people cause to invest their emotions in taking sides. There are some notorious jerks both living and dead who definitely deserve our venom. But are they actually evil, or are they stupid? To understand why we hate, we must develop a sense of the difference between evil and stupidity. Further, we need to define what sets stupidity apart from our casual definitions of the word. If you are willing to spend a little time and energy, we shall learn something.

### An example from history.

Let's take an historical example of a person whose exploits are being closely, and perhaps knowingly, mirrored by many of today's right-wing politicians: [Benito Mussolini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benito_Mussolini). Yes, the Italian guy from World War II (Adolf Hitler is too notorious of an example, most people would choose _not_ to try and understand his motivations). He was a man who seemed immune to logic. Instead of listening to his detractors, he jailed them, or worse. 

Regardless of the circumstances predating Mussolini's rise to power and descent into fascism, we cannot place the blame solely upon him alone. As his self-anointed sobriquet says, he was _Il Duce_, the leader. For one to lead, others must follow. He had lots of help from some very intelligent people. People who, without closer examination, were acting irrationally and easily manipulated. For an evil movement to succeed, there must be a critical mass of people willing to forego their critical thinking skills and submit to the absurd.

### An axiom of evil.

To develop our theory that Mussolini's followers were truly stupid, we must first establish a rule. An axiomatic definition of the man to give us a baseline against which we can measure his followers. The axiom is this: Mussolini was evil. How was he evil? Political murder, assassination of rivals, atrocities in Ethiopia, Greece and Yugoslavia, and participation in the holocaust by rounding up Italian Jews and sending them to German death camps. He also closed opposition newspapers and banned public protest meetings. He declared all political parties illegal except for his own Fascist Party. He outlawed labor unions and strikes. He also established a political police force, assholes in black shirts set loose on the streets, to ferret out antifascists. Let's face it, the guy was a dick. 

We have the advantage of hindsight. Mussolini’s enablers did not have the advantage of foresight. Some of them may have been smart, but they could not predict the future. That wasn’t important to them, anyway. But now we have a problem: how can we describe the type of people who would support an evil dictator as intelligent? We start by not confusing stupidity with foolishness, ignorance, or malice. 

If I walk my dog without a leash, and he attacks a cyclist, I am a fool. It was foolish of me to not think ahead and use a leash. Not minding your surroundings is foolishness, not stupidity. If I go to London, where cars drive on the left, I should look right when crossing the street. But I am from California, so I naturally look left. Never having learned something is ignorance, not stupidity. If I desire to hurt others, I am malicious, not stupid.

Mussolini’s followers were enabling a dictator while ignoring the pain and suffering he was inflicting on the Italian people. Intelligent people can  certainly be foolish, ignorant, or malicious. As humans with a capacity to understand, they had the ability to change their bad behavior. Stupidity, on the other hand, is a whole other box of frogs. When people slide into the abyss of the ridiculous, it can be dangerous. More dangerous than evil.

### Prison gives you time to think.

Overexposure aside, I need to switch to Nazi Germany for a moment. Around the same time Mussolini was in Italy being the evil asshole he was, a man named [Dietrich Bonhoeffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietrich_Bonhoeffer) was in prison in Germany[^1]. He was a vocal critic of the Nazi regime and wrote many books and papers refuting the efficacy and credibility of the German government which he labeled an "idolatrous cult of the _Führer_." Before he was hanged in 1945, he wrote many letters from his cell in [Tegel Prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tegel_Prison). He wrote that evil wasn't the most dangerous thing:

>Stupidity is a more dangerous enemy of the good than malice. One may protest against evil; it can be exposed and, if need be, prevented by use of force. Evil always carries within itself the germ of its own subversion in that it leaves behind in human beings at least a sense of unease. Against stupidity we are defenseless. Neither protests nor the use of force accomplish anything here; reasons fall on deaf ears; facts that contradict one’s prejudgment simply need not be believed – in such moments the stupid person even becomes critical – and when facts are irrefutable they are just pushed aside as inconsequential, as incidental. In all this the stupid person, in contrast to the malicious one, is utterly self satisfied and, being easily irritated, becomes dangerous by going on the attack. For that reason, greater caution is called for when dealing with a stupid person than with a malicious one. Never again will we try to persuade the stupid person with reasons, for it is senseless and dangerous. (Bonhoeffer, 2010)

### Identifying stupid.

We can take our axiom of evil, apply it to Mussolini, Hitler, Pol Pot, whomever. That's fine. Now we have to a little work to do. When reason falls on deaf ears, how do we know that those ears belong to a stupid person? Remember, we are neither dealing with foolishness, ignorance nor malice. We are not examining evil specifically here. How do we develop our theory of intelligent people acting stupidly? What can we learn from men like [Galeazzo Ciano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galeazzo_Ciano), who could have led a life far different from the one he married into? Or maybe [Marcello Piacentini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcello_Piacentini), an urban theorist and architect, responsible for designing Italian fascist buildings and monuments. I would argue that despite their circumstances, they were intelligent people who had a lot of potential[^2]. 

Author and philosopher [Jonny Thompson](https://bigthink.com/people/jonnythomson/), logically defines this in his article [Bonhoeffer’s “theory of stupidity”: We have more to fear from stupid people than evil ones](https://bigthink.com/thinking/bonhoeffers-theory-stupidity-evil/). In the [video companion](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HbQ-t8GXYTA) to this article, Thompson lays out Bonhoeffer's three conditions of stupidity:

1.**Outsourcing critical thinking:** Surrendering one's independent judgment to an external authority, political slogan, mass movement, or technology rather than engaging with reality firsthand.

2.**Willful ignorance and conformity:** A strong psychological need to belong to a group, causing individuals to align their identity with the crowd and willingly give up their autonomy for a false sense of certainty.

3.**Resistance to facts:** An active, stubborn immunity to reason or contradictory facts, where the "stupid person" is possessed by catchwords and becomes dangerous when confronted with the truth.

First, technology (something that I have many issues with) has enabled us to do things that we would not normally do in the presences of others. We have all at one time or another, outsourced our critical thinking by having so much information at our fingertips, ready to access for free. Despite our advances in technology, we are getting busier. It only makes sense to give up part of our critical thinking potential to technology so we can prioritize what is important to us. Thus the first condition above is almost inescapable at times.

As the second condition notes, we all want to be part of the group. We are all willing to suspend certain beliefs in order to belong. That is part of being human. We organize ourselves by religion, musical taste, political leanings. Grouping together is how we as humans have survived through ice ages and famines.

The third condition of stubbornness is, in my opinion, the one that truly turns an intelligent person stupid. 

>The fact that the stupid person is often stubborn must not blind us to the fact that he is not independent. In conversation with him, one virtually feels that one is dealing not at all with a person, but with slogans, catchwords and the like, that have taken possession of him. He is under a spell, blinded, misused, and abused in his very being. Having thus become a mindless tool, the stupid person will also be capable of any evil and at the same time incapable of seeing that it is evil. (Bonhoeffer 2010)

For example, labeling anyone who speaks out against fascism as “antifa,” or repeating “Make America Great Again” _ad nauseum_. Shouting about free speech while denying it when confronted by facts. Times have certainly changed in America. Social media has made it easier to be a mindless tool. Creating a post that is racist, denialist, or paranoid is a lot easier than dealing with the public’s perception directly. 

[Carlo Cipolla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlo_M._Cipolla), in his 1976 essay [_The Basic Laws of Human Stupidity_](https://web.archive.org/web/20130216132858/http://www.cantrip.org/stupidity.html)[^3] identified five ways to identify stupidity. His message about the dangers of stupid people is similar to Bonheffer’s:

>Essentially, stupid people are dangerous and damaging because reasonable people find it difficult to imagine and understand unreasonable behavior. An intelligent person may understand the logic of a bandit. The bandit’s actions follow a pattern of rationality: nasty rationality, if you like, but still rationality. (Cipolla 1976)

The only way to fight stupid people is to understand them. We cannot do that until we know what motivates them. To fight what we hate, we must first uncover it and understand it. As Cipolla argues, reasonable people are unable to recognize stupidity before it is too late. Although the stupid may be intelligent, they are ignorant and blind to their own vulnerabilities. They are, as Bonhoeffer would argue, manipulated by the evil person, used up, and sometimes sacrificed for the evil person’s evil ends.

>Bonhoeffer’s argument is that the more someone becomes part of the establishment, the less an individual they become. A charismatic, exciting outsider, bursting with intelligence and sensible policies, becomes imbecilic the moment he takes office. It’s as if, “slogans, catchwords and the like… have taken possession of him. He is under a spell, blinded, misused, and abused in his very being. (Thompson 2023)

### Stupidity never pays.

As for _Il Duce_, it turned out that the Italian people have their limits. As the Americans and British were advancing through Italy, Mussolini, his lover and her brother fled with as much as they could carry. Rather than stick to his convictions and face the music, Mussolini, like others of his ilk, turned yellow and ran for his life. He was eventually captured and shot.

If we can understand how intelligent people can do very stupid things, maybe we can work toward creating a world in which men are truly measured by their actions (and thus, face consequences) regardless of their intelligence. 

>And so it would seem that stupidity is perhaps less a psychological than a sociological problem. It is a particular form of the impact of historical circumstances on human beings, a psychological concomitant of certain external conditions. (Bonhoeffer, 2010)
