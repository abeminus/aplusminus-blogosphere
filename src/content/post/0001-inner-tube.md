### The backstory.

My mother forbade the riding of bicycles in the rain because it was super dangerous. I tried to avoid it anyway because I simply could not stand being all wet and sweaty. Riding a bicycle usually makes me happier in any situation save one. Riding a bicycle in the rain, does nothing to cancel out the swampy feeling of involuntarily taking a shower outside with your clothes on. The only way that scenario could be worse, is when said bicycle fails mechanically. Situations where the rain is falling, misery is compounding, and tools and supplies are absent can only be described as rock bottom. 

To wit: in the winter of 1982, despite a mild weather forecast, I found myself in a situation very similar to the above. I was riding home from school on my Schwinn Predator BMX bike when I was caught in the rain. I lived about a mile and a half from the school, and the ride usually took me about fifteen minutes. The skies were darkening as I unlocked my bike from the racks. It is a quick ride, I thought, and that I would be fine. I wasn’t. I was also eleven years old and I had a lot of rage. So what turned out to be a light shower was interpreted by me as a biblical deluge.

I was riding up the hill near the school when it began to rain. It was unusual for rain to begin so suddenly. There were no warning drops, no wind, just rain. It was like someone just turned on the sprinklers only it wasn’t summer and I wasn’t watering the grass. I often have trouble articulating my disdain for water. I don’t hate water in general. I grew up in a beach town where I surfed and swam in the ocean. I enjoyed rainy days especially right after the rain stops and the air is super clear and I can hear the water dripping off the trees. But it is different while riding a bicycle. Only then is water pure evil.

Everything was super shitty and I was engrossed in my hatred for precipitation when, as I crested the hill towards downtown, I noticed something. What I had thought was a series of rough patches in the road was more rhythmic than normal. There was a feeling coming through the saddle as if I was running over a roll of dimes over and over again. It grew in intensity and the intervals slowed as I slowed to a stop to confront my fear. I had a puncture in my rear tire and the air was slowly escaping. I could hear it, only faintly during the intervals of cars passing. My rage at the weather soon became a full-blown nuclear meltdown at nature itself. The earth, it seemed, strategically placed a thorn in the path of my rear tire. 

It seems that Mother Nature, with her twisted sense of humor, casually decided to grow a thorn and just fling it onto the surface of the bike lane, one pointy side facing up. Just then, I recalled a memory of my father telling me about goat’s head thorns while hiking the previous summer. We were in the back country hiking near the old quicksilver mine when he pointed out these plants at the side of the trial. They were about knee high and had these tiny little yellow flowers. These, my dad said, will dry up and form a thorn with four spikes. One spike always sticks straight up. 

I never considered that thorns would be on the city streets, just waiting for the opportunity to make my life miserable. But back then, I did not know what to do about a flat tire. I was happy in my ignorance and I had always treated my bicycle as a never-changing tool to get from my house to wherever. However, when my bike was defeated by a former flower, my whole world was different. 

Flat tires were the end of the world. I was eleven, after all. A bicycle with a flat tire was simply not usable. It was an inert hunk of metal and rubber. After getting home and drying off, I watched TV until my parents got home. I told my father about my experience and he asked to see my bike. He had a smile emblazoned across his face because he saw this as a teaching moment. Besides, he didn’t really have time in the mornings to drive me to school. Fixing the flat tire was worthwhile tofor both of us.

Eleven-year-old me would have never believed that fixing a punctured tire at home was possible. Weren’t there whole stores with mechanics who got paid to do that? It is part of the human experience to have to deal with something that we’ve never encountered. Something as small as a stupid thorn grown by a stupid plant on the side of the road can have a big effect.

### My rage persisted, but is not visible.

Let’s talk inner tubes. This is not meant to be a step-by-step guide for changing a punctured tube. This article is a deep dive into the mind of a bicycle mechanic. It is a long-form, verbose output of everything you should be thinking about while restoring your punctured bicycle to a usable state. Below will be everything an extremely talkative mechanic would teach to a non-mechanic, given that either aren't busy and willing to spend the time. 

A couple more things to keep in mind: This article is not meant to teach you what to do in an emergency. You should either be about to go for a ride, or have just finished one. You should have access to bicycle tools and a bicycle pump. A bicycle repair stand is a plus. This article also assumes that you have removed the wheel with the problem tube. There are many different ways a bicycle wheel may be attached to its frame. Go figure out how take off your wheels before continuing.

I will also not be including instructions on how to patch an inner tube and reuse it. Patching a punctured tube should only be attempted in an emergency situation (when you have no replacement tubes and are stuck in the middle of nowhere), or you are not in a position financially to acquire a new one. But seeing as Murphy and his ridiculous laws exist, I highly recommend carrying a patch kit with you when you ride. Learn how to use it, and tuck it into your tool bag. Oh yes, get a tool bag for your bike. 

Is the wheel off your bike? Good. Let's start with the basics: consider why you want to change the inner tube. Is it necessary to convert the old tube’s position from inside the tire to the outside of the tire? Asking these questions and answering them will ultimately save you time, especially if the tube just needs to be inflated. In which case, you needn't have bothered to remove the wheel in the first place.

I will give you the benefit of the doubt and say that the chances of you being so inept that you feel any deflated tube needs to be changed are super low. However, if you removed the wheel from the bicycle already, congratulations. You are well on your way to getting back on your bike and riding it. For the sake of due diligence, try to inflate the tube. Attach a pump and give it a few strokes. But wait...did you prepare to change the tube without having access to, or owning a bicycle pump? If that's the case, take the wheel to your local bike shop, have them change the tube. While you're at it, purchase a pump.

Moving on. You should have a pump with the correct valve interface (we'll get to those later), and a new tube of the correct size (we'll get to that later as well), and a tire lever. You are almost there. But standing there, looking at your wheel with the pump in one hand and the lever in another only signals your readiness, not your knowledge. Before we begin, let's go over some basic bicycle terminology. I do not mean to be pedantic, to mansplain, nor pretend that I am better than you. But confusion with the terms will only hamper your efforts. Here we go:

### Bicycle wheel terminology.

+ ***Wheel.*** The bicycle wheel consists of a tire, a tube, a rim, rim strip, spokes, and a hub. The whole round thing, one of a pair, that lets your bicycle roll along. Most people will know their wheel by its size: i.e. 700c, 650b, 26”, 27.5”, 29”, 20”, etc.

+ ***Tire.*** The rubber thing that attaches to the rim and makes contact with the road. The tire had two beads which connect it to the rim, so it stays put. Tires come in diameters and widths, and are rated for certain air pressures.

+ ***Tire lever.*** A small plastic tool designed to separate the tire from the rim via the tire bead interface. Usually there is a hook on one end and a flat, spoon-like shape with a dull edge on the other end. Plastic is better than metal as it is less likely to puncture the tube or damage the bead of the tire.

+ ***Tube (or Inner Tube).*** The rubber donut-shaped thing that holds the air and goes inside the tire, between the tire and the rim. The air that pressurizes a tire goes in the tube via a valve that pokes through the the valve hole in the rim.

+ ***Valve.*** The part you attach the pump to. Air goes from the pump, through the valve into the inner tube. There are two popular types, Presta (long and skinny) and Schrader (like the one on your car).

+ ***Rim.*** The round part of the wheel that the tire attaches to. There may be a braking surface for rim brakes to contact, right next to the edge near the tire beads.

+ ***Rim strip.*** A flat, tape-like strip made of plastic, rubber, or fabric that keeps your inner tube from pushing its way through the spoke holes in the rim, preventing it from rupturing. 

+ ***Spokes.*** The long, thin metal pieces that attach the hub to the rim to keep it round.

+ ***Hub.*** The center, spinning part of the wheel that attaches the wheel to the bike. There may be a disc brake rotor attached here if you have a disc-brake bicycle.

Looking at these definitions, you may now realize that when someone says "I have a flat tire," what they really mean is "I have a flat tube." But it's not even really flat, is it? It's donut shaped and flaccid. The terminology is important, but not as important as the meaning. As such, we will be focusing on the tire, tube and rim (and maybe the rim strip if necessary).

### How the sausage is made.

Hold your bicycle wheel with both hands so that the side of the wheel is facing you. Look at the side of the tire. Is there writing printed on it? Maybe there is a manufacturer's name and model. If you can see this, you are looking at the sidewall of the tire. There should also be information printed or embossed on the rubber. Usually, there are numbers denoting the tire's size and air pressure range, in pounds per square inch, or PSI. Examples of a tire size would be 700 x 28C, 26" x 1.95", et cetera. Examples of a tire's air pressure range would be something like 35 - 55 psi, or max. 110 psi. Once you have this information, you should have a tube to match the size of the tire with the correct valve type (the air pressure is less important for most tubes).

Now locate the valve. This is the long, metal and/or rubber thing that sticks through the inside of the rim, pointing toward the hub, between the spokes. The valve is the interface between the pump and the tube. Simply stated, air goes through it. The two most popular types are: Presta, found on bikes with narrow rims and high-end mountain bikes, are long, thin, metal affairs, usually with threads to accommodate a valve lock ring or an adapter. Schrader valves, usually found on wider rims and less costly bicycles, are exactly like the valves on a car tire, and are thicker than the Presta valves. Both valves can use a valve cap, designed to keep out dust and moisture. Many cyclists discard the caps.

For a Presta valve, unscrew the small, knurled piece at the very tip of the valve. It is a captive screw, designed to remain on the valve. Unscrew it enough to get a couple of millimeters of play, then press down towards the valve. If you have any air remaining in the tube under pressure, it should escape. If not, make sure it moves in and out freely. For a Schrader valve, take a pen, screwdriver, or key and press in on the small metal post in the center of the valve tip. Both of these procedures should release any remaining air left in the tube. With your attention fully fixated on the valve, open it to let any remaining air out. That's it. Get it all out.

Again, unless you are a total idiot, you need to take note of not only the valve type, but whether there is any remaining air left in the tube. Wait...you say there is air in the tube? Does that mean  you can just re-inflate it and be on your merry way? Not so fast. You could have a slow leak, in which case the non-idiots would have paid close attention to how frequent the tube needed inflation. For the sake of thoroughness, take a look at the outer surface of the tire, especially the portion where the rubber meets the road. Look closely at the treads and scan for anything that looks like it doesn’t belong. Thorns, nails, shards of glass, and tiny pieces of wire can all be culprits. If you see something, note its position on the tire and move on to changing the tube, If you don’t see something obvious, let us just assume that we need to change the tube at this point, shall we?

Good. Now on to the fun part. Time to separate the tire from the rim. This may require some effort. Do some stretching exercises if you must. You may break a sweat.

Grab the wheel with one hand and place it on the floor, perpendicular to your shoulders as if you are about to roll it away from you. I recommend straddling the wheel and keeping it locked between your heels so it doesn’t move. With your other hand, left or right, it doesn't matter, grab your tire lever. Ignore the hook end. The dull, flat-edged tip is what will be used to separate the tire bead from the rim. With the thumb and fingers of the wheel-grabbing hand, try your best to pinch the tire so the bead moves away from the rim towards its center. If the tire bead does not separate from the rim easily, you may need to press harder with your thumbs.

Now, take the tire lever, and shove it into the gap. Try to wedge the spoon-like edge underneath the tire bead and pry it over the edge of the rim, taking care not to grab the tube or the rim strip in the process. Keeping the lever in place, try to slide it away from you along the circumference of the rim, separating the rest of the bead on that side. Rotate the wheel so you can keep sliding the lever without falling on your face. Once the entire bead on the lever side has been released, stop. Admire your work. You are now almost halfway done. Please do not relax at this point.

Now place the wheel in front of you, parallel to your shoulders as if you were to roll it away from you to the left or right. Find the valve again and make sure it is on top, tip facing toward the floor, away from your face. Make sure the free, unattached tire bead is facing away from you. Put the lever down and grab the free bead with the fingers of both hands and gently pull it toward you over the valve portion of the tube. This part can be tricky as the rubber surrounding the valve is thicker and stiffer than the rest of the tube. 

Once the tire in this section is out of the way, the valve can be pulled up through and away from the rim. If you have a Presta valve, remove the small knurled lock ring that is near the rim before attempting to pull the valve stem through the hole in the rim. If you have a Schrader valve, just pull. The rest of the tube can now slide out of the tire away from you. Do not allow the wheel to rotate during this procedure. You need it to remain in the same position so you can locate the source of the puncture on the tire.

Look at the tube surface that was formerly against the inner surface of the tire. Look for any holes. If there are none visible, check the base of the valve stem near the tube itself. Are there any cracks or holes there? You can also check the surface of the tube which would be against the inner side of the rim. No visible holes? Try adding some air. Listen for any air that escapes. This is where not allowing the wheel to move from its original position comes in handy. Fill the tube with air and listen and feel around for the puncture. There should be air escaping from it. Fill it up a lot, tubes can stretch into comically large donuts.

Once you have found the hole in the tube, take a note of where it is relative to the valve. Is it on the outer side or the inner side relative to the rim? If the hole is on the outer side, the one that contacts the inner surface of the tire, feel along the inner surface of the tire with your free hand at about the same distance from the valve hole and see if there are any thorns or sharp pieces of metal. If you find some, pull them out. I cannot emphasize enough how important it is to remove every piece of foreign matter from the tire. Failure to do this will result in a new puncture. If the hole is on the rim side of the tube, check the rim itself, your rim strip may be compromised.

Every rim has holes in it to allow the spokes to be connected. If a tube contacts these holes under pressure, the edge of the hole will puncture the tube. A rim strip acts as a barrier to prevent the tube from squeezing through the holes. If your rim tape is torn or shifted away from the holes, this could be your problem. There are many causes of rim tape failure. One of the most common is shifting due to not keeping your tube up to pressure. If you reinflate your tube from a very low pressure, it could shift the rim strip. Either reposition or replace the rim tape. Rim tape comes in many different styles, sizes, and material. Replace it with the exact type you had before.

Once you are satisfied with the state of your tire and rim strip, you are ready to insert a new tube! You can choose to take a break, have a snack, or keep going. The choice is yours.

Before we insert the new tube, we need to do something that has absolutely no bearing on the function of the wheel, but is otherwise taboo. Most lay people do not know this, but it is totally uncool to not have the label printed on the sidewall of the tire not centered with your valve.  If it is not, slide the tire around so that the center of the label lines up with the valve hole in the rim. Having a well-aligned tire make you look like you know what you are doing. Cyclists and mechanics can be very picky. Even though what you choose to do with your bicycle does not affect them, they will point out all the flaws in your decisions.

Once you are ready, grab a hold of your new tube. Take it out of its box and unroll or unfold it. Remove the cap, valve lock ring and unscrew the valve nut if you have a Presta valve. Before inserting the tube into the tire, add some air to give the tube some shape. Don’t add too much, just enough to make it look like a skinny donut. Position the wheel like it was before, parallel to your shoulders, valve hole at the top and the free tire bead facing away from you. With your free hand, gently pull the free tire bead toward you (toward the other tire bead), exposing the valve hole in the rim. Insert the new tube's valve through the hole in the rim until it stops. Then pull the tire bead back over the tube and away from you. Tuck the rest of the tube into the tire, going around the wheel with both hands. Let some air out of the tube if you need to, as it may make fitting it all in difficult. Ensure that the tube is well seated between the tire beads.

Stop. Let’s pause for a moment and think: how do I attach the tire bead back onto the rim? There are two ways, or preferences, among bicycle mechanics: tuck in the tire bead nearest the valve and work your way towards the other side, or start at the other side and go towards the valve. Anecdotally, I believe that most mechanics choose to start at the valve. But I could be wrong. There is a valid argument for starting on the opposite side of the valve. Some mechanics do so in order to give the tire beads more room to slide into the center of the rim, making it easier to seat the beads. The truth is, it really doesn’t matter as long as the end result is a clean installation. Let's begin with the part of the bead nearest the valve.

Now with the wheel still in position parallel to your shoulders, free bead facing away, start by tucking in the tire bead nearest the valve between the tube and the rim edge. Let some more air out of the tube if necessary. With both of your thumbs, move around the wheel towards the opposite side, tucking in the tire bead as you go. I cannot stress the importance of making sure that you do not pinch the tube in between the tire bead and rim. Make sure the tube stays fully in between the two tire beads! If there is any hint of a pinch, a catastrophic failure will occur when you pressurize the tube. You will not need to look closely for a hole after it goes boom.

Bring the wheel up level with your hips, tucking as you go around. As you reach the opposite side of the rim, the bead will give you more and more resistance. If you can pop it all the way on, great. If you reach a point where it seems impossible, try a few things before you reach for the tire lever for help.

First, completely deflate the tube and try seating the last part of the bead again. If you have already done that, or it doesn't seem to help, try settling the tire into the center of the rim. Using your thumbs and fingers, go around the entire tire and pinch toward the center of the rim. The point of this is to bring the tire beads down into the channel of the rim where they have more room, thus giving the beads more slack. If that doesn't work, set the wheel against your hips, with the unseated portion of the tire bead facing up and away from your body. Then, with both hands flat against the unseated bead, curl your fingers around the top of the tire and with a rolling motion. try and get the bead over the rim.

Unless you are trying to seat a very tight, narrow road tire, or a tubeless tire, the above should work without resorting to using a tire lever to pry the bead over the edge. If you absolutely must use your tire lever, check to make sure you aren't pinching the tube as you force the bead over the edge. This happens a lot. Even though you may not tear a hole, you can leave a small amount of tube pinched between the bead and the rim, causing the catastrophic failure mentioned above.

There are a few things left to do before you add air to the tube. Start by placing the wheel on the floor in front of you, parallel to your shoulders. With your thumb on the rim surface facing you, use your fingers to pull the bead toward the center of the rim, and check the resulting gap for any tube rubber that my be pinched. Do this on both sides, going around the entire wheel.

Next, check the valve. Make sure that it is not at an angle coming out of the hole. If it is, it means the tube has shifted and adding pressure may press the base of the valve against the valve hole, possibly compromising the tube. Try to shift the tire so that the tube is dragged to a point where the valve is no longer at an angle. This is very important. If you cannot shift the tire, you must start over. I know, I know, I wouldn't want to begin again, but it is better safe than sorry.

Once you have determined that the valve is angled properly and the tube is not pinched between the tire beads and the rim, get ready to add some air. Take the valve between your thumb and forefinger and push it toward the rim, so that some of it gets pushed into the tire. This is necessary to clear the tube rubber around the valve, which is thicker, away from the tire beads. If you have a Presta valve, thread the lock ring onto the valve, but not all the way down.

Place the wheel against a wall or workbench leg with the valve on top, facing down. Attach the pump to the valve. Remember, if you have a Presta valve, make sure the knurled cap has been loosened before attaching the pump. Start pumping slowly to make sure the air is moving freely into the tube. If the pump is not attached properly, it will soon become obvious. Either the air will hiss out, or it will be blocked entirely.

For the sake of safety, check the label on the tire for the pressure range. For example, let's imagine we are inflating a road tire with a maximum pressure of 110 psi. If your pump has a gauge, fixate on it while pumping. The pumping should be very easy. Add about 20 psi and check the beads. If they are still clear and not pinching, add some more air.

As the tube is pressurized, the tire beads will press against the rim. The beads are shaped so that they will lock into a channel on the inner side of the rim edges. If you hear some pops or creaks, as you increase the pressure, do not be alarmed. Slow down on the pumping and let the beads settle in. As you get close to the maximum pressure of the tire, it should get more difficult to operate the pump.

Now would be a good time to check the seating of the tire beads. Is the bead in the same position around the circumference of the rim? Before fully pressurizing the tube, detach the pump and spin the wheel. Does the tire look like it has a flat spot. Does it look warped in any way? If it does, there may be areas where the tire beads are not seated properly. Take a look at the sidewall and see if there are any areas where it looks like the tire is tucked too far into the rim. If this is the case, let all the air out and massage the tire beads into the correct place. Attach the pump and begin slowly adding air again.

Here is where you need to make a decision regarding your riding style. Ask yourself: do I need to have the maximum pressure in the tire? If the maximum is 110 psi, is 100 psi okay? It depends on the application. For road bikes, I would say get as close to the maximum pressure as you feel comfortable doing. A harder tire will result in less rolling resistance and fewer punctures. For mountain bikes, you need to consider the state of the trails you plan to ride. A lower pressure and softer tire will give you more traction. Too soft, you risk a pinch flat if you hit a rock with your wheel too hard.

Now that you have filled your tire to your desired pressure, let the wheel sit for a moment to make sure it isn't deflating slowly. Check the firmness of the tire with your hands. Go get yourself a burrito and a drink. Kick back and bask in the glory of your accomplishment.

### Don't be afraid to do the work yourself.

One of modern society’s biggest problems is that we have lost our ability to fix even the simplest things. I do not deny that changing the inner tube on a bicycle is simple. Far from it. But it is something that the bicycle manufacturers have not paywalled yet. Unlike many things in this world, a bicycle is still repairable by the end user. All it requires are some special tools and a little knowledge and time.
