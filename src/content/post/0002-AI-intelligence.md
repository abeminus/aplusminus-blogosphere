###  Introduction

Many companies are investing in AI technology and trying to lure investors by adding some sort of human-like intrface to their product. If you have heard people complain about AI being ever present in their daily lives, there is a reason. The term _artificial intelligence_ is a misnomer. The technology is definitely artificial, but it is not even close to being intelligent. Knowledgeable, sure. But in a way that a dictionary knows a bunch of words and definitions. It is an ordered list of words and their meanings that is easily searchable and applicable to human endeavors. However, a dictionary is not intelligent. It cannot think. It is not capable of abstract thought. The technology that is on a four-comma scale (trillions) of dollars is not _intelligent._

### It sure seems to be.

Of course it seems intelligent. Anything that mimics human speech and mannerisms can give a person the impression that it is thinking. But just stop for a second, and really think about the word _think._

As a human being, in a physical body made of all kinds of weird fluids and tissues, the only thing I know about thinking is that I do it all the time. At least I _think_ I do. I don’t really know, and many philosophers have been asking themselves the same question. Thinking is wrapped up in all kinds of discourse about things like intelligence, consciousness, rationality, and even religion. But for each individual, we have our own definition. 

### Connections to the outside world. 

Let’s spend a little time talking about _belief._ It doesn’t matter what our definition of belief is, we just know that belief is an important part of our identity. If we put all the things we believe in into a list, that forms a dataset. We can then parse that set of things into a verbal or written output that can be mundane or extremely powerful to other humans. But is that what we are actually doing? Are we just using a set of data to produce thought? We can certainly make a large language model (LLM) output something similar based on tables of data and probabilities. But the machine cannot _feel_ the result. Humans can have a visceral connection to the words they hear. This connection to the words leads to a connection to the speaker. Empathy. Sympathy. Pity. Excitement. Pride.

A machine’s physical connection to the outside world is possible. It can be connected to all sorts of sensors to analyze and produce output of its environment. The AI inside can indeed make something that resembles a human when they say “I feel cold,” or “I think there is a gas leak.” However, I believe that only a human is capable of getting a “cold” feeling from another person. Only a human can offer the possibility of a gas leak when a friend farts loudly in a crowded theater. Our physical connection to this world goes way beyond merely sensing our environment.

Consider the five basic senses, that is sight, hearing, touch, smell, and taste. We can imagine them to be merely the input of various sensors spread throughout our bodies. And certainly we can construct a clump of code that can mimic the output of sensors as comprehensible speech. But it is doubtful that an AI can say what its sensory input _means_ for its output. If an AI smells gas, can it consider that someone nearby ate spicy food for lunch? If it could make that leap, would it be disgusted?

### Connections to time.

Throughout human history, there has been much debate as to whether there is a sixth sense. Although we have not reached an official consensus, I think our sense of time is a good candidate. Remember that I mentioned we are made up of all kinds of weird fluids an tissues? It’s true. We are disgusting. Especially when we get older. We age. The passage of time has a profound effect on who we are as people. Each person ages in different ways physically. Each person handles their age in different ways psychologically. We cannot avoid the passage of time. We see the seasons change. We feel our bodies breaking down. Why does my neck hurt so much? 

An AI construct is not concerned with this. Please bear with me on this next nugget of declaration: for an AI, a computer, a machine, _time does not exist._

We will never win our battles with time. Because we are such losers at fighting with time, it is an important factor in everything we do. We spend it. We waste it. We can have plenty of it. We can certainly run out of it. A computer executes code within the confines of its system clock. The high and low voltages, the ones and zeroes only appear when the clock is cycling. A computer can be old, but it cannot _feel_ age. I have an old computer that I use for writing. One day, it will cease to function. Regardless of the time it has left in this world, it executes code. That’s it. If it is executing code 15 years from now (yeah, right), that 15 years of experience has no bearing on its output. In this sense, time does not exist for a machine. 

### Abstract thought.

There is one thing that AI excels at, that is making connections in its dataset. The models that we use in academic research, medicine, mathematics, and physics can seem almost miraculous. Finding new treatments for cancer, solving centuries-old theorems, these are the things that AI can do well. It finds connections in a set of data that humans haven’t stumbled across.

Imagine for a moment that you have thousands of sounds and music samples from across all eras and genres. You can then take these parts of music and mix them into something that sounds completely different. This is what DJs and music producers have been doing for years for decades. First, they used tape, then electronic sampling. These days, anyone with a computer can use software to make music in their own home. Here’s the thing: so can AI. LLMs can take huge libraries of sounds and music and create original pieces merely from prompts. Pretty cool, right? Well, sort of. The machines, in this case, are merely making connections exactly like the music producers of old used to do. 

If you have heard any of the AI-generated “music” that has clogging up your streaming feed, you are no doubt familiar. It also sounds...okay. In a sense, it is original, but it is nothing so awesome it knocks your headphones off. Now imagine you are scrolling through your feed, and you find something that the feed’s algorithm has _not_ suggested. It sounds like nothing you’ve heard before. It is totally different and not based on any previous musical convention. Is it a whole new genre? Is it even music? What is this and why am I crying?

A machine cannot do this. A machine cannot come up with something original that is not based on its datasets. This is what is known as _abstract thought._ And AI is not capable of it. It cannot be because of the core principals that allow an LLM to operate: they can only work on data that they have previously recorded, ordered, and assigned a probability to. Abstract thought requires either creating new data, or changing data from one thing to another.

Albert Einstein changed how we think of space and time by creating space-time, a new thing that materialized from his biological neurons and brain chemistry. Imagining space like a fabric that bends and warps near large masses was more than just connecting different types of math together. Space-time was born in his mind, _outside_ of convention. It is something that had never come before. We can also say the same things about quantum mechanics. That was Max Plank making an assumption. Machines don’t do that. An AI would never ask about cats in boxes with poisoned food when no one had ever asked that before.

If I were to feed an LLM all of human medical knowledge up to the year 1900, would it be able to come up with the molecular structure of DNA, or the cure for polio? Probably not. [James Watson and Francis Crick, et al.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_Structure_of_Nucleic_Acids:_A_Structure_for_Deoxyribose_Nucleic_Acid) (DNA, 1953) and [Jonas Salk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonas_Salk) (polio, 1955) each had at least five decades on our poor LLM...it would not be capable of finding those solutions because of the huge half-century gap of medical advancement in its dataset.

### Conclusion.

Mimicry can get very close to an appearance of intelligence. If the software and hardware is good enough, many of us can be fooled into thinking they are talking to a person and not a machine. Companies are likening their LLMs to magic boxes. They are black boxes to be sure. But keep in mind that mimicking human thought is also mimicking its errors. Indeed, the error rate to power consumption ratio is getting worse: we are throwing massive amounts of electricity and compute power towards [solving errors at an ever decreasing rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_scaling_law). There’s nothing magic about that.

It all comes down to that distinction between knowledge and intelligence. When I was younger, we called it book smarts versus street smarts, or common sense. We can teach a machine many things by loading up its dataset and letting it find connections. It can find many ways to use that data differently. However, it will never be able to come up with its own unique data on its own. So If something has no real physical connection to the outside world, doesn’t age, and is not capable of abstract thought, we should never believe that it is capable of thought at all.
